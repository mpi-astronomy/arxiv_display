<div id="title">

# An ALMA view on molecular complexity: water, methanol, and methyl formate in the HD 100546 and IRS 48 disks

</div>
<div id="comments">

[![arXiv](https://img.shields.io/badge/arXiv-2610.22503-b31b1b.svg)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22503)<mark>Appeared on: 2026-10-19</mark> -  _Accepted for publication in the Astrophysical Journal. 21 pages, 9 figures, 3 tables_

</div>
<div id="authors">

L. Rampinelli, et al. -- incl., <mark>M. Benisty</mark>

</div>
<div id="abstract">

**Abstract:** Water and methanol are the fundamental constituents of interstellar and cometary ices: understanding their abundances in disks is therefore of high importance to reveal how this ice reservoir evolves across the timeline of star and planet formation. We present ALMA band 5 line emission observations of water and methanol in the warm Herbig disks of HD 100546 and IRS 48. While the IRS 48 disk is methanol-rich, water vapor is not detected, implying a high methanol-to-water column density ratio $>0.2$ , possibly reflecting a distinct ice composition. Methanol and water are both detected in the HD 100546 disk, leading to a $\ce{CH3OH/H2O}$ ratio of $\sim0.02$ , consistent with the median of the cometary distribution and with the lower edge of the distribution of Young Stellar Objects. We suggest that the gas-phase methanol-to-water ratio in the HD 100546 disk directly reveals the ice reservoir subsequently sublimated in the warm disk stage. Moreover, we report a methyl formate-to-methanol ratio in the IRS 48 disk of $\sim0.2$ , consistent with the upper edge of the distribution of Young Stellar Objects. The methyl formate-to-methanol ratio in the HD 100546 disk is $\sim2$ , much higher than in younger and older sources, possibly reflecting a chemical enhancement of gas-phase methyl formate. We additionally constrain non-LTE conditions, optical depths, and emitting regions of the methanol lines in both disks. These results offer a new opportunity to probe the chemical trail of molecular complexity in the intermediate disk stage, bridging early and late phases of the star and planet formation history.
